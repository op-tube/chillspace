Social Media is Actually Boring

Let's be honest with ourselves for a moment. We've all been there. You open an app, your thumb instinctively hovers over the feed, and then you scroll. And scroll. And scroll. But here's the thing nobody wants to admit: social media is actually boring. Not in the way a blank wall is boring — more in the way a slot machine is boring once you've seen the mechanism behind it. It's predictable, repetitive, and ultimately hollow. And yet, we keep coming back, because the machine has been very, very well built.

The TikTok Paradox: You'd Almost Always Rather Do Something Else

Let's start with the elephant in the room, or rather, the elephant on your screen. TikTok has been called the most engaging platform ever created, and that's probably true. But "engaging" is not the same as "enjoyable." In fact, I'd argue that there is always something you would rather do than watch TikTok. Always. Even the most dedicated TikTok user, if they're being honest, would rather be on a beach, at a concert, having dinner with friends, or even just reading a good book in silence. The reason TikTok feels compelling is not because it's good — it's because it's easy. And there's a world of difference between the two.

The deeper problem is the fundamental structure of the feed. When you watch TikTok there are bad and good videos, mixed together in an unpredictable stream. You swipe past three mediocre clips to find one that makes you laugh or teaches you something interesting. But the math is brutal: for every genuinely entertaining video, you've wasted minutes of your life on content you didn't care about, didn't ask for, and will never remember. It's a lottery where the prize is a brief moment of mild amusement, and the cost is your attention, your time, and your energy.

Hypothesis #1

It would be better to only watch the good ones. This sounds obvious, but think about what it implies. If you could guarantee that every video you watched was genuinely entertaining, informative, or moving, would you still waste time on a random feed? Of course not. You'd watch exactly the content you love and then move on to something more meaningful. The fact that platforms don't offer this is not a technological limitation — it's a business decision. They want you scrolling through the bad videos, because that's how they keep you on the app longer, show you more ads, and collect more data.

And let's not forget the alternatives. People often say "well, what else would I do?" as if TikTok is the only option. But look around: YouTube has decades of high-quality, curated, long-form content that respects your time. Netflix offers entire seasons of storytelling crafted by professionals who actually care about narrative arcs and character development. And then there's ChillSpace — our social media simulator — which takes a completely different approach. Instead of an endless feed of algorithmic guesswork, ChillSpace gives you a curated, intentional social experience that doesn't treat your attention as a resource to be mined. The point isn't that any single alternative is perfect. The point is that the "there's nothing else to do" excuse is exactly that — an excuse, and a weak one at that.

Celebrity Social Media: Different Faces, Same Trends

Now let's talk about "celebrity social media" — that strange, glossy corner of the internet where famous people post carefully staged photos, share snippets of their glamorous lives, and pretend to be "just like you" while living in a mansion you'll never set foot in. Here's the uncomfortable truth: "celebrity social media" is just different faces doing the same trends. Scroll through any celebrity's feed and you'll see the same dance challenges, the same lip-sync videos, the same "get ready with me" routines, the same sponsored content for the same beauty brands. The faces change, but the content doesn't.

This is not a coincidence. It's the result of an ecosystem that rewards conformity over creativity. When a trend emerges, every celebrity — or more accurately, every celebrity's social media manager — rushes to replicate it, because that's what the algorithm rewards. Originality is risky. Trends are safe. And so we end up with a feed full of beautiful, famous people doing the exact same thing as everyone else, with slightly different lighting and a different caption. It's not content; it's content filler. It's the digital equivalent of elevator music — pleasant enough in the background, but actively boring if you actually pay attention.

Big Tech's "Post and Video Creation Machine"

And this brings us to the uncomfortable truth about how the entire system works. Behind the glossy surface of social media is a cold, hard business model. Big Tech is using creators, and not in the way you might think. It's not just about underpaying them for views or taking a cut of their sponsorship deals — it's about treating them as a "post and video creation machine". Creators are the raw material, the fuel that keeps the engine running. Without a constant stream of new content from millions of creators, the platforms would have nothing to show, nothing to recommend, and nothing to sell ads against.

“ Big Tech has built a machine that turns human creativity into feed content — but the machine runs on human minds, and those minds are burning out.

The creators themselves are often unaware of how thoroughly they've been integrated into this machine. They think they're building a personal brand, expressing themselves, connecting with fans. But from the platform's perspective, they're just nodes in a vast content production network. The platform doesn't care about the individual creator's voice, vision, or artistic integrity. It cares about one thing: volume. More posts, more videos, more content, more engagement, more ad impressions. The creators are the gears, and the platform is the machine, and the output is a never-ending river of content that flows past your eyes and disappears into the void.

The Boredom Was Always There — You Just Didn't Notice

So here we are. Social media is actually boring. It's boring because it's repetitive. It's boring because it's predictable. It's boring because behind every viral trend is a corporate machine that has figured out exactly how to keep you scrolling without ever giving you anything of real substance. It's boring because there is always something you would rather do than watch TikTok, and deep down, you know it. It's boring because when you watch TikTok there are bad and good videos, and you're wasting your time on the bad ones just to get to the good ones, when it would be better to only watch the good ones — or better yet, to watch something else entirely, on YouTube, on Netflix, or on ChillSpace.

It's boring because "celebrity social media" is just different faces doing the same trends, and the glamour wears off the moment you realize that everyone is just following the same playbook. It's boring because Big Tech is using creators as a "post and video creation machine" — grinding out content at an unsustainable pace, burning through human creativity like it's a renewable resource, when it's not.

And most importantly, it's boring because there's a better way. ChillSpace proves that you don't need billions of creators to have a social experience. You just need a few lines of code, a clear vision, and the courage to say: we don't need to exploit human minds to entertain people. "Programming a few lines of JavaScript instead of programming billions of human minds" isn't just a clever tagline — it's a fundamentally different philosophy about what social media should be.

“ The most radical thing you can do on social media is admit that it's boring — and then go do something that isn't.

So the next time you find yourself reaching for your phone out of habit, pause for a second. Ask yourself: Is this actually enjoyable, or am I just scrolling because I'm bored? If the answer is the latter — and let's be honest, it usually is — close the app. Go do something real. Watch a movie that was actually crafted with care. Read a book. Call a friend. Or come hang out on ChillSpace, where we've replaced the endless scroll with something a little more intentional.

Social media is actually boring. It's time to admit it — and then move on.
